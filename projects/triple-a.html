Authoring Accelerator Agent

Keeping TurboTax's Digital Assistant authoring going after the expert team behind it offboarded

Client: Intuit ProTax Group (TurboTax) Year: 2025–2026 Deliverables: Local search tool (Python + HTML), system prompt, weighted scoring system, 768-utterance golden dataset

Overview

TurboTax's Digital Assistant answers millions of customer questions, and someone has to write and check those answers. That work was being done by a borrowed team of tax experts, and on April 16 they were going back to their regular jobs. Nobody had a plan for what happened after that.

I built Authoring Accelerator Agent before that date. It takes real customer questions (we call them utterances), searches 3,684 articles across TurboTax help, Tax Tips, and IRS content, and writes a response that follows our conversational style guide, with sources, a confidence rating, and a note on any content gaps. The expert team needed 2 business days for 100 utterances. The tool does 100 in under 5 minutes, and about three out of four answers come back ready for a light review instead of being written from scratch.

Customers never see this tool. It's what let the Digital Assistant content team keep working after the experts left.

The Problem

Keeping the Digital Assistant accurate is ongoing work. Someone takes real customer utterances, checks whether we have the right content to answer them, writes responses that follow the Intuit Conversational Design Style Guide, and flags missing content for the roadmap.

Seven tax experts were doing that three days a week (Monday through Wednesday), with two to four leads reviewing their work. It wasn't their main job. At that pace, 100 utterances took two full business days, and after tax season the experts were leaving.

The tooling was its own problem. The team had tried a Gemini Gem connected to NotebookLM notebooks as a starting point. The notebooks disconnected at random, the Gem pulled web results even when told to use only our content, and the formatting changed from run to run. It worked in a demo and fell apart in daily use.

Speed was part of it, but the bigger issue was that the whole process depended on people who were about to leave, and nobody had planned for the day after.

How It Works

The tool has three layers. The first is a Python pre-filter that runs instantly. It works out what kind of question it is (a tax question, product support, navigation, or refund status), picks out IRS form numbers like 1099-R, 1098-T, or Schedule E, routes the question to one or two topic groups, and scores every article in those groups. The top 10 move on.

The second layer is Claude Code, called from the script. It gets a system prompt I wrote, about 3,000 words of content design rules, citation formats, and Digital Assistant guidance from the Intuit Conversational Design Style Guide, along with the 10 articles and the customer's question. It returns a search log, a suggested response, citations with excerpts and links, a confidence level (HIGH, MEDIUM, or LOW), and a summary of any gaps.

The third layer is a single-file web page that shows the result. It color-codes confidence, adds the best source link to the response automatically, and has a batch mode with a progress bar and history. It also cleans up after the model: it moves the response to the same spot every time and fills in a confidence level when Claude leaves it out. I added both because prompt instructions alone weren't reliable enough.

The Scoring System

The pre-filter is more than keyword search. A form-number match adds 200 points, and a near-miss form from the wrong family subtracts 200. IRS content loses points on product support questions. Multi-word phrases, matches in the title, and rarer words all count for more. Anything under 35 points is dropped, and if nothing clears that bar, the tool says "Sufficient content not available in corpus" instead of offering a wrong article.

That cutoff mattered. Without it, the tool always returned something, even an article that shared one common word with the question. For a writer, an honest "nothing here" is more useful than a confident wrong answer.

The System Prompt Was the Design Work

Most of the important design decisions on this project live in the system prompt. It's about 3,000 words, and it defines what the tool is and what it produces.

It says the tool is a content search engine and not a tax advisor. It sets which of the three sources to prefer, the exact citation format, what each confidence level means, how to label links (catalog, IRS reference, or a warning for web results), and how responses should read: plain, neutral, two to four sentences, and always ending with the best link. Those response rules come straight from the Intuit Conversational Design Style Guide, so the style guide is now something the tool follows on every run.

Some instructions that looked clear on paper didn't hold up. Claude wouldn't reliably include a link, and it put the response in different places. Instead of rewriting the prompt over and over, I moved those jobs into the web page's code. The model handles the writing, and the code handles the things that have to be exactly the same every time.

Three Tries Before One That Shipped

Try 1: Gemini Gem with NotebookLM. I split 3,390 articles into 7 topic notebooks and built a Gem to search them, going through 3 versions of the prompt. The notebooks kept disconnecting (a known bug), and the Gem kept pulling web results anyway. Not usable day to day.

Try 2: A static web app calling a model API. I built it and put it on GitHub Pages. The design was fine, but I couldn't get an API key. The Anthropic console was locked down, our OpenAI enterprise account had no credits, and Intuit's internal platform needed permissions I didn't have. It worked and nobody could use it.

Try 3: A local Python server plus Claude Code. I realized I could call Claude Code from a script. A small Python server does the pre-filtering, serves the page, and sends prompts to Claude under each person's own login. Everyone runs it on their own machine, so there are no shared keys and no server to maintain. This is the one that shipped.

The API-access problem that killed the first two never went away. Changing where the tool runs made it stop mattering.

Measuring Quality: The Golden Dataset

The tool includes a /golden command for Claude Code that runs 100 to 1,000+ utterances in parallel batches. I used it to build a 768-utterance golden dataset and tracked how the confidence results changed from version to version.

Metric V1 (Initial) V4 (Current)
HIGH confidence 72.0% (553) 55.6% (427)
MEDIUM confidence 24.9% (191) 21.0% (161)
LOW confidence 3.1% (24) 23.4% (180)

HIGH confidence dropped a lot between V1 and V4, and that was the right result. V1 had no way to say "I can't answer this," so it rated wrong-topic answers as HIGH. V4 added the minimum score, so when nothing relevant exists it says so. The 156 utterances that moved from HIGH to LOW are real gaps in our content, not the tool getting worse.

With AI tools, a higher number isn't automatically better. You have to know what the number is measuring.

Impact

100 utterances used to take the expert team 2 business days. The tool does them in under 5 minutes.

The bigger change is how much needs a full expert review. With 76.6% of answers at HIGH or MEDIUM confidence, experts only need to write about 1 in 4 from scratch. The rest come back with citations, source excerpts, and a style-guide-ready response for a quick check. That split is what kept the work going after April 16.

  • 2 business days down to under 5 minutes for 100 utterances
  • 76.6% of answers ready for a light review instead of full authoring
  • 50 utterances in about 3 minutes in batch mode, and 1,000+ overnight in parallel
  • A 768-utterance golden dataset, evaluated across 4 versions
  • Anyone on the team can run it, with no API keys or shared server
  • Content gaps come out as structured data the content roadmap can use

Since Then

The tool has kept growing. I've built a shared team service version so people won't need their own setup, and it's working its way through Intuit's deployment approvals. It has an opt-in catalog of 831 expert-only TurboTax articles, and it now covers professional tax too: one engine with separate consumer and ProTax profiles, with the ProTax side searching about 7,000 FAQs, expert articles, and product notices. After that change, I checked that consumer results stayed the same across 1,944 utterances.

What I Took From It

A lot of the content design here doesn't look like content design. The system prompt is a content deliverable, the scoring rules are editorial judgment written as code, and where the tool runs was a UX decision that shaped every teammate's experience with it.

The deadline also forced some honesty. The experts weren't going to stay, API access wasn't going to show up in time, and more prompt tweaks weren't going to fix the Gemini setup. So I stopped waiting on those and built around them. The tool shipped before April 16, the documentation lets anyone run it, and the golden dataset gives the team a way to re-check quality as the content changes.
